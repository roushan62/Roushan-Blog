How to Start a Franchise Business in India: Costs, Profit Margins and the Questions Nobody Tells You to Ask

A franchise is often described as a safer way to start a business. You get an established brand, a proven process, training and supply chain support. What the brochures rarely mention is that you also inherit someone else's rules, someone else's margins, and a contract you will live with for five to nine years.

This guide walks through what a franchise actually costs in India, how the money is split, which categories perform, and the specific questions you must ask a franchisor before signing anything.

A realistic minimum for a small food outlet in a tier-2 city is ₹25–40 lakh all-in. A well-known national quick-service brand in a metro location can cross ₹1 crore before the first customer walks in.

Key Takeaways

  • The franchise fee is the smallest part of the cost. Interiors, equipment, deposit and working capital together are usually three to five times larger.
  • Royalty is calculated on revenue, not profit. A 6% royalty on a business running at 4% net margin consumes more than your entire profit.
  • Location decides outcomes more than brand. A mid-tier brand on a good road beats a top brand in a bad lane.
  • India's food and beverage market alone is estimated at around $94 billion in 2026 and projected to grow at a double-digit rate — which is exactly why franchising is expanding fast, and why bad franchisors also multiply.
  • Before signing, speak to at least three existing franchisees who are not on the franchisor's referral list.

Editor's Note: Cost ranges here are indicative market figures as of 2026 and vary sharply by brand, city and outlet size. Always obtain the franchisor's written disclosure document and have a lawyer review the agreement — India has no single franchise law, so the contract is the only protection you have.

What a Franchise Actually Is (and Is Not)

A franchise is a licensing arrangement. The franchisor owns the brand, the operating system and the supply relationships. You own the outlet, the staff and the risk. You pay for the right to operate under their name and follow their method.

What you are really buying is three things:

  1. Demand you did not have to create. Customers already know what to expect.
  2. A system you did not have to invent. Recipes, pricing, staffing, layout, supplier rates.
  3. Training and ongoing support. The value of which varies enormously between brands.

What you are not buying is guaranteed profit. That part remains entirely yours to earn or lose.

Which Categories Perform in India

Food and beverage

The largest and fastest-growing segment. India's foodservice market is estimated at roughly $94 billion in 2026, heading towards $150 billion-plus by 2031, with cloud kitchens growing fastest of all formats and travel hubs — airports, railway stations, highway plazas — recording the highest growth rates. Delivery is expanding faster than dine-in.

The catch: food has the highest wastage, the most staff turnover and the thinnest margins. A good food franchise needs the owner present, especially in the first year.

Retail and apparel

Lower daily operational intensity than food, but higher inventory risk and heavy dependence on location and season. Unsold stock is the silent killer.

Education and preschools

Strong in tier-2 and tier-3 cities, where branded preschools command parent trust. Long gestation — it typically takes one to two admission cycles to fill — but very high retention once established.

Health, pharmacy and diagnostics

Steady, recession-resistant demand. Regulated, which means licensing takes time but also protects you from casual competition.

Salons, gyms and wellness

Membership models generate predictable cash flow. Success depends almost entirely on local marketing and staff retention.

Services — courier, laundry, repairs

Lower investment, faster break-even, but also lower ceilings. Good first franchises for someone learning the business.

How the Money Actually Works: A Sample Unit Economics

Take a hypothetical food outlet doing ₹12 lakh of revenue a month:

Line item % of revenue Amount
Revenue100%₹12,00,000
Food and material cost32%₹3,84,000
Staff18%₹2,16,000
Rent14%₹1,68,000
Utilities and maintenance6%₹72,000
Aggregator commissions10%₹1,20,000
Royalty6%₹72,000
Marketing fund2%₹24,000
Net before tax12%₹1,44,000

On an investment of ₹45 lakh, ₹1.44 lakh a month is a payback of roughly 31 months — before any tax, and assuming nothing goes wrong. That is a reasonable franchise outcome. Now reduce revenue by 20% and the net margin falls to near zero, because rent, staff and royalty do not shrink with your sales.

This is the single most important thing to understand: franchise economics are brutally sensitive to volume.

Break-Even: The Calculation You Must Do Yourself

  1. List every fixed monthly cost: rent, salaries, royalty minimums, EMIs, insurance, software.
  2. Calculate contribution margin per order or per customer: price minus variable cost.
  3. Divide fixed costs by contribution margin. That number of customers per day is your survival line.
  4. Stand at the location on a Tuesday and a Sunday and count footfall. If the survival line is more than 40% of realistic footfall, walk away.

The 20 Questions to Ask a Franchisor Before Signing

  1. How many outlets have opened and how many have closed in the last three years?
  2. Can I speak to three existing franchisees I choose, not ones you suggest?
  3. What is the average monthly revenue of your five worst-performing outlets?
  4. What is the exact royalty percentage, and is there a minimum guarantee?
  5. Which suppliers am I forced to buy from, and at what rates compared with the open market?
  6. Who decides the selling prices — me or you?
  7. What is the territory protection radius, in writing?
  8. What happens if you open a company-owned outlet next to mine?
  9. How much marketing spend do you commit to my city, and how is it measured?
  10. What training is included, for how long, and at whose cost?
  11. What are the renewal terms, and has the fee changed at renewal before?
  12. What are the exit and transfer conditions? Can I sell to a third party?
  13. What are the penalties for missing brand standards, and how are they enforced?
  14. Do aggregator orders (Swiggy, Zomato) attract the same royalty?
  15. What technology and POS system is mandatory, and what does it cost monthly?
  16. Who handles customer complaints and refunds on delivery orders?
  17. What is the realistic break-even timeline based on your last twenty openings?
  18. Is the brand's trademark registered, and in which classes?
  19. Are there any ongoing legal disputes with existing franchisees?
  20. What exactly happens if the franchisor is sold or shuts down?

Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

  • Guaranteed return promises. No franchisor can guarantee your revenue.
  • Refusal to share contact details of existing franchisees.
  • Pressure to sign quickly because "the territory is being offered to someone else".
  • A high franchise fee but no detail on training, support or marketing.
  • A brand with many outlets opened and many quietly closed in the same period.
  • Unwillingness to put the marketing commitment in writing.

Funding a Franchise in India

  • MUDRA loans — collateral-free lending for micro and small enterprises, with the Tarun Plus category going up to ₹20 lakh. Suitable for smaller formats.
  • Bank term loans — most public and private banks have specific franchise-finance products; expect to offer the outlet assets or property as security.
  • Equipment leasing — reduces upfront cash for kitchen and machinery, at a higher effective cost.
  • NBFC loans — faster but more expensive; useful for bridging, dangerous as permanent capital.

A sensible rule: fund at least 40% from your own capital, and keep six months of operating costs untouched. Under-capitalisation, not bad locations, closes most franchise outlets in their first year.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a franchise really safer than starting my own brand?

Safer in demand creation — you do not have to teach people what you sell. Not safer financially. You carry the same rent, staff and volume risk, plus a royalty, and with less freedom to change what is not working.

What is a realistic payback period?

For a well-run food or retail franchise in a good location, 24 to 40 months. Anything promising under 12 months is marketing.

Do I need experience in the same industry?

Not usually, but you need the ability to manage people. Most franchise failures are operational, not strategic — staff absenteeism, inconsistent quality, untracked inventory.

Can I run a franchise from another city?

Only with a manager you trust completely and a system you check daily. Absentee ownership is the most common reason outlets underperform.

Is the franchise agreement regulated in India?

There is no dedicated franchise law. The agreement is governed by the Indian Contract Act and related commercial law, which is why the contract terms matter so much and legal review is not optional.

Final Word

A franchise is a way to buy a system, not a way to avoid work. The people who succeed are the ones who treat it like their own business — present at the outlet, obsessive about quality, and ruthless about the numbers. The people who fail are the ones who thought a famous logo would do it for them.

Do the unit economics on paper before you visit a single showroom. Then talk to three franchisees who have nothing to gain from your decision. Those two steps will save you more money than any discount a franchisor offers.
