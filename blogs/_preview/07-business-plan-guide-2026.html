How to Write a Business Plan in 2026 That Investors and Banks Will Actually Read

Written by Roushan Gupta • 8 min read • 1,764 words
Table of Contents auto-generated by your theme

    Nobody reads a forty-page business plan. Not an investor with two hundred decks in their inbox, not a bank manager with a loan queue, and not your future self six months from now.

    That does not mean you should not write one. It means you should write the right one: a document short enough to be read, specific enough to be believed, and structured so that the three questions every reader actually has — what is the opportunity, why you, and how does the money work — are answered in the first two pages.

    This guide gives you the exact structure, the numbers you must include, and the mistakes that get plans rejected.

    Total: ten to twelve pages. Everything else goes into an appendix.

    Key Takeaways

    • Write the executive summary last, but place it first. It decides whether the rest is read at all.
    • A bottom-up market size — customers you can name, price you will charge, share you can realistically reach — beats a top-down "India is a $500 billion market" claim every time.
    • Your financial model needs three years, not ten. Anyone projecting year seven is guessing, and readers know it.
    • Traction, however small, is worth more than projections. Ten paying customers outranks a beautiful forecast.
    • A bank and a venture capitalist want different documents from the same business. Write two versions.

    Section 1: The Executive Summary

    One page, five paragraphs, no jargon.

    1. The problem, with a number attached. "Independent tuition teachers in Lucknow lose 6–8 hours a month chasing unpaid fees."
    2. The solution, in one sentence a twelve-year-old could repeat.
    3. The market, sized bottom-up.
    4. Traction — the strongest fact you have. Revenue, users, waitlist, pilot customers, letters of intent.
    5. The ask — how much, for what, over what period, and what it gets you to.

    Test it on someone outside your industry. If they cannot summarise your business back to you in one sentence after reading, rewrite it.

    Section 2: Problem and Customer

    Do not describe a category. Describe a person.

    Weak: "Small businesses in India struggle with digital payments." Strong: "Rakesh runs a mobile repair shop in Kanpur with four employees. He handles about 90 orders a month, records them in a paper register, and by his own count loses around ₹18,000 a month to unpaid follow-ups and forgotten advance payments."

    Include evidence that the problem is not invented:

    • How many customer conversations you conducted, and what they said (quote them)
    • What they currently pay for the workaround
    • Why existing solutions fail them

    Section 3: Market Size, Done Honestly

    The "TAM / SAM / SOM" format is fine, but the numbers must be buildable from facts you can defend.

    Approach Example Credibility
    Top-down (avoid) "India's education market is $117 billion, we need 0.1%" Low — 0.1% of anything sounds achievable and means nothing
    Bottom-up (use) "There are roughly X tuition centres in Lucknow. We can reach 400 in year one at ₹1,200 per month." High — each number can be checked

    State your assumptions openly: number of reachable customers, realistic conversion rate, average price, expected churn. A reader who can see your assumptions can argue with them — which is a far better conversation than one where they simply disbelieve you.

    Section 4: Competition — Including the "No Competition" Trap

    Never write "we have no competitors". Every reader translates that as "this founder has not looked, or there is no market".

    Your real competitors are usually:

    1. Direct competitors — companies doing the same thing.
    2. Indirect competitors — different tools solving the same problem.
    3. The status quo — a spreadsheet, a notebook, or doing nothing. This one wins more often than any startup.

    Build a simple grid with the five attributes your customer actually cares about, mark where each competitor stands, and show honestly where you are still weak. Admitting one weakness increases belief in every other claim.

    Section 5: Go-To-Market — The Section Most Plans Fudge

    "We will use digital marketing and social media" is not a plan. A go-to-market section needs:

    • Channel: which one, specifically. Field sales in Lucknow, LinkedIn outreach to CAs, WhatsApp communities, Amazon listings.
    • Cost per customer: your best estimate, and how you arrived at it.
    • Sales cycle: how many days from first contact to payment.
    • The first 100 customers: name the exact communities, cities or accounts. If you cannot, you do not have a plan yet.
    • What compounds: referrals, content, partnerships, distribution deals.

    Section 6: The Financial Model

    Three years, monthly for year one, quarterly for years two and three. Build it in a spreadsheet; paste the summary into the plan.

    The eight numbers every reader looks for

    Number Why it matters
    RevenueVolume × price. Show both, separately.
    Gross marginShows whether the business can ever work
    Customer acquisition costThe single number that kills most early models
    Lifetime valueAnd the LTV to CAC ratio — aim above 3:1
    Monthly burnHow fast the money disappears
    RunwayMonths until you run out
    Payback periodHow many months to earn back the CAC
    Break-even monthWhen revenue covers costs

    Three scenarios, not one

    Show a base case, a downside case (half the conversion, double the CAC), and an upside case. The downside case is the one investors study, because it shows whether you understand your own fragility.

    Section 7: Traction and Milestones

    Traction is anything that proves the world wants this:

    • Paying customers, however few
    • Repeat usage or retention numbers
    • Signed letters of intent from businesses
    • A completed pilot with a named institution
    • A waitlist with a real conversion rate, not just a number of emails

    Then set the next three milestones with dates and the metric that will prove each one. Specificity here is what separates a plan from a wish.

    Section 8: The Ask

    Be precise:

    • How much you are raising or borrowing
    • What it buys, line by line — hiring, marketing, inventory, equipment
    • How many months of runway it provides
    • What you will have achieved at the end of it

    Avoid "for general working capital". It signals that you have not decided.

    Government Schemes Worth Knowing Before You Write

    Your plan gets stronger if it aligns with available support. As of 2026:

    • DPIIT recognition is the entry point — it unlocks tax benefits, easier compliance and eligibility for most schemes. India has more than 2.35 lakh recognised startups, with roughly half coming from tier-2 and tier-3 cities.
    • Startup India Seed Fund Scheme offers up to ₹20 lakh as a grant for proof of concept and prototyping for recognised startups under two years old. The ₹945 crore corpus has been committed through more than 200 incubators.
    • Credit Guarantee Scheme for Startups covers collateral-free debt up to ₹20 crore through member banks and NBFCs.
    • MUDRA provides collateral-free loans for micro-enterprises, with the Tarun Plus category going up to ₹20 lakh.
    • Fund of Funds 2.0 is a ₹10,000 crore vehicle aimed at deep tech and long-horizon ventures.

    Mention in your plan which of these you intend to apply for and why you qualify. It shows you have done the homework.

    The Ten Mistakes That Get Plans Rejected

    1. Forty pages when twelve would do.
    2. No named customer — only a category.
    3. "We have no competition."
    4. Top-down market sizing with a fantasy percentage.
    5. Revenue projections with no explanation of how customers are acquired.
    6. Ignoring customer acquisition cost entirely.
    7. Ten-year financial forecasts.
    8. No downside scenario.
    9. A vague use of funds.
    10. Zero traction and no plan to get any before the next raise.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Do I really need a business plan to start?

    No. You need one to raise money, borrow money, or bring in a partner. To start selling, you need customers. Many successful founders wrote their first real plan only when a bank asked for it.

    Business plan or pitch deck — which first?

    Write the plan first, in plain language. The deck is then a ten-slide summary of it. Doing it the other way round produces slides with no substance behind them.

    How detailed should the financials be?

    Monthly for the first twelve months, quarterly after that. Three years is enough. Every number must trace back to an assumption you can defend in conversation.

    Should I include AI and technology buzzwords?

    Only if they are true and specific. "AI-powered" without an explanation of what the model does is now a negative signal, not a positive one.

    Can I use AI to write my business plan?

    Use it for structure, for stress-testing assumptions, and for tightening language. Never let it invent your market numbers or your financials — those must come from you.

    Final Word

    A business plan is not a document you write once and file. It is a thinking tool. The real value comes from the moment you try to write the customer acquisition cost and realise you have no idea what it is — that discovery, before you spend money, is worth more than any template.

    Write it short. Write it honestly. Update it every quarter. And let the numbers, not the adjectives, do the persuading.
