How UPI Actually Makes Money: The Economics Behind India's Free Payments

On 25 August 2026, the Unified Payments Interface turned ten. In the financial year 2025–26 it carried 241.62 billion transactions worth about ₹314 trillion — an increase of more than 4,000 times in value since its first year. In July 2026 alone, UPI processed a record 23.66 billion transactions worth nearly ₹30 lakh crore, according to data from the National Payments Corporation of India.

And the customer paid nothing for any of it.

This is the part that confuses people outside India: how does a payments system process over 760 million transactions a day for free? A credit card swipe charges a merchant 1.5% to 3%. A UPI scan charges most merchants zero. Both move money. Only one of them charges for it.

Here is the actual economics, and why the two systems were never competing in the first place.

Whose money moves Your own money, from your bank account The bank's money, lent to you for a billing cycle

Key Takeaways

  • Credit cards are a lending business disguised as a payments business. Most of the profit comes from interest and fees, not from the swipe.
  • UPI is a public-utility rail. It was designed for financial inclusion, and it deliberately does not try to earn on each transaction.
  • UPI's monthly volume crossed 23 billion transactions in 2026 — roughly 760 million transactions a day — at an average value around ₹12,000 to ₹13,000 per transaction.
  • Because UPI transactions are so small and so free, no private company could have built the economics on merchant fees alone. The scale is the strategy.
  • UPI is not killing credit cards. RuPay credit cards linked to UPI now carry around ₹10,000 crore of credit transactions a month — the two systems are merging.

Editor's Note: Volume and value figures come from NPCI's published monthly data as reported in 2026. Fee structures are set by the RBI and the NPCI and have changed several times — always check the latest circular before relying on a specific MDR number.

First, a Correction: These Are Not the Same Product

Comparing UPI to a credit card is like comparing a road to a car loan. One is infrastructure for moving money you already have. The other is a credit facility that happens to have a payment mechanism attached.

Understanding this is the key to the whole economics question. When you tap a credit card, three things happen at once:

  1. The merchant receives money (minus a fee).
  2. The bank lends you that money for up to roughly 45–50 days.
  3. If you do not repay in full, the bank earns interest at an annualised rate that typically lands between 36% and 42%.

When you scan a UPI QR code, only the first thing happens. There is no loan, so there is no interest to earn.

Where Credit Card Money Actually Comes From

1. The merchant discount rate

When a customer swipes, the merchant typically pays around 1.5% to 3% of the bill. That is split between the issuing bank (which gets the largest share), the card network, and the acquiring bank that installed the machine. On a ₹10,000 purchase, roughly ₹200 disappears before the shopkeeper sees the money.

2. Interest on revolving balances

This is the real engine. A customer who pays only the minimum due can end up paying 36–42% annualised. A single revolving customer is often worth more to a bank than several hundred merchants' swipe fees.

3. Fees

Annual fees, joining fees, late payment fees, cash withdrawal fees, foreign currency markup, EMI conversion charges. Individually small, collectively enormous.

4. Rewards as a marketing budget

Cashback and air miles are not generosity. They are a customer acquisition cost that also increases how often people use the card — and every use either earns MDR or pushes someone closer to revolving.

Where UPI Money Comes From (Or Doesn't)

The merchant pays nothing

Person-to-merchant UPI payments carry zero MDR. This was a deliberate policy choice, not an oversight — the goal was to make digital acceptance possible for a tea stall, not just for a shopping mall.

So who earns?

Several parties, indirectly:

  • Banks earn on the float, on the savings accounts they now hold, and on cross-selling. A customer who opens an account for UPI is a customer who can later be sold a loan, insurance or a credit card.
  • Payment apps earn on lending layered on top, on merchant subscriptions, on bill payments, insurance distribution and advertising inside the app. The payments themselves are the customer acquisition channel.
  • Merchants earn by accepting more customers, selling faster, and avoiding cash handling — which is why they accept zero fees willingly.
  • The economy earns through formalisation. Every digital transaction creates a record, and records create creditworthiness.

Where the government stepped in

UPI's zero-fee model put real financial pressure on the banks that operate it, since they bear the infrastructure cost without per-transaction revenue. The government has periodically provided subsidies to offset transaction-processing costs for smaller banks and operators, recognising that the benefit is systemic rather than commercial. This is exactly what a public utility looks like: costs socialised, benefits socialised.

The Scale Numbers, and Why They Matter

June 2026 22.72 billion ₹28.92 lakh crore Lower only because June has 30 days

Do the arithmetic on July 2026: ₹30 lakh crore across 23.66 billion transactions gives an average transaction value of roughly ₹12,600 — and that average is pulled up by large person-to-person transfers. The everyday tea-stall payment is often under ₹100.

Now apply a 1.5% card-style fee to a ₹30 transaction. You get 45 paise. No private company can run a nationwide real-time settlement network on 45 paise per transaction. The only way to make it work is to remove the fee and monetise something else — which is precisely the design choice India made.

Why Credit Cards Still Win Where They Win

UPI's dominance has not erased credit cards, because credit cards solve a different problem:

  • Credit. A ₹60,000 phone purchase on EMI is a loan, not a payment. UPI cannot do that from a savings account.
  • Large tickets and travel. Hotels, airlines and international merchants prefer cards for chargeback protection and pre-authorisation.
  • Rewards economics. For someone who spends ₹1 lakh a month and pays in full, a good card genuinely returns value that UPI does not.
  • Credit building. A well-used card builds a credit history, which lowers the cost of a future home loan.

The Convergence Nobody Predicted

The most interesting development is not competition but merger. RuPay credit cards can now be linked directly to UPI, so a customer can scan any UPI QR code and pay from their credit line. According to NPCI's leadership, credit transactions through this route run around ₹10,000 crore a month, with credit lines on UPI accounting for a small but growing slice.

This changes the economics fundamentally:

  1. The merchant still pays nothing on the UPI leg.
  2. The bank now earns interest on the balance, because it is a credit transaction.
  3. Credit cards get UPI's distribution — hundreds of millions of QR codes instead of a few million card machines.

In other words, India did not defeat credit cards. It gave them a rail they could never have built themselves.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is UPI really free for merchants?

For person-to-merchant payments, yes — there is no MDR. Some specific categories, wallet-to-bank transfers and certain credit-line transactions carry charges. The rules are set by NPCI and the RBI and have been revised several times.

Who owns UPI?

UPI is owned and operated by the National Payments Corporation of India, an umbrella organisation promoted by the Reserve Bank of India and the Indian Banks' Association. It is not a private company.

Do banks lose money on UPI?

On the transaction alone, they earn nothing and bear the processing cost — which is why the government has provided periodic subsidies. They recover value through deposits, cross-selling and the broader formalisation of the economy.

Why can't other countries copy UPI?

They are trying, and several have. But copying requires three things at once: a national identity and mobile infrastructure, banks willing to share a common rail, and a regulator able to force interoperability. India had all three.

Will UPI start charging fees?

Person-to-person UPI has always been free. Person-to-merchant has zero MDR as a policy choice. Any future change would be a political and economic decision, not a technical one — and it would be fiercely resisted by merchants.

Final Word

Ten years after launch, UPI has proved something that most payment economics textbooks said was impossible: a real-time national payments network can run at enormous scale with no per-transaction fee, because the returns are systemic rather than commercial.

Credit cards still earn more per transaction by a factor of hundreds. UPI still moves more money than anything else in the country. Both models work — they just answer different questions. One asks, "How do I monetise this payment?" The other asks, "How do I make payments so cheap that everyone uses them?"

India chose the second question, and got a payments revolution instead of a payments business.
